# 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)

## Learning objectives

Measure text contrast against its background and apply WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.3 (Level AA): https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#contrast-minimum.

## Reading against the background

Low-contrast text can be difficult to read for people with low vision, color vision differences, or reduced contrast sensitivity. Contrast is a ratio based on the relative luminance of foreground and background; a higher ratio means a greater difference. Measure the actual colors as displayed, including transparency and overlays. Color names and appearance alone are unreliable.

Normal text generally needs at least 4.5:1. Large-scale text needs at least 3:1: approximately 18 point regular or 14 point bold, or equivalent larger sizes. Do not round 4.49:1 up to 4.5:1. Boldness and font rendering matter, so use a suitable tool and check the actual rendered text. Text over a gradient or image must be readable against the backgrounds behind it; use an opaque or sufficiently darkened background when the image varies.

The criterion has exceptions for incidental text, including inactive user interface components, pure decoration, incidental text in a picture, and text not visible to anyone; logotypes are also excepted. Read the exact definitions before applying an exception. Disabled controls can still raise usability questions even where their text is excepted. Other criteria address contrast of non-text components and state indicators.

## Example and evaluation

```css
.notice {
  color: #000;
  background-color: #fff;
}
```

Black on white has a 21:1 ratio. In a real design, inspect links, placeholders, errors, button labels, and hover and focus states. Measure the text against the adjacent background, including the least favorable part of a varying image. Browser zoom and operating-system settings may change presentation; inspect those experiences as well. Passing a color checker alone does not establish page conformance.

## How to measure a real interface

Start with the actual foreground and background colors rather than a design token name. Inspect the rendered element in the browser, then use a contrast checker that calculates the WCAG ratio. If the text is translucent, its effective color depends on the background beneath it. If a background contains an image or gradient, a single sample may not represent the worst area behind the letters. An opaque text panel or overlay can make the result more predictable; measure the final combination rather than the intended colors alone.

The boundary matters. A normal text measurement of 4.49:1 does not meet 4.5:1 even if a tool rounds its display. Similarly, a measured 2.99:1 does not meet 3:1. Use sufficient precision and verify that the tool is evaluating the correct color pair. Anti-aliasing can affect the apparent edges of letters; follow the WCAG definition and the checker’s documented approach instead of choosing a lighter pixel to obtain a favorable result.

## Worked examples and exceptions

A card displays a small gray price on a white background at 3.7:1. It fails the normal-text threshold even though the large card title reaches 3.2:1 and may satisfy the large-text threshold. Darken the price text or adjust its background and remeasure. Do not treat a whole card as one test: every relevant text/background combination and state needs evaluation.

A button disabled while an operation is unavailable may fall within the incidental-text exception for inactive components. Verify that it really is inactive, rather than merely styled to look disabled while remaining usable. Incidental text, pure decoration, text in a picture that is incidental, text not visible to anyone, and logotypes have specific provisions. The full criterion and definitions decide whether an exception applies. Even where a criterion excludes disabled text, make its state and reason understandable when that helps users complete a task.

## State and context checks

Measure default, hover, focus, active, visited, selected, and error states when they alter text colors. Check placeholders and text inside controls; placeholder text should not be the only label because it disappears on entry. A link that meets contrast against its background may still rely on color alone to distinguish it from surrounding text, so review 1.4.1 too. Likewise, a visible button label may pass while the button boundary does not meet non-text contrast requirements.

Document the text, foreground and background, ratio, size, weight, state, threshold, and any exception. After changing a color, retest neighboring components and states, because the same token may be shared. Automated scanning can identify many candidate failures, but gradients, dynamic states, and context often need manual review.

## Design review: a status banner

A warning banner has dark orange text on a pale orange background. The heading is 20 CSS pixels and bold, while a smaller explanation uses the same colors. Measure both against the banner, recording their font size and weight. Do not assume the heading qualifies as large simply because it looks prominent: compare its computed size with the large-scale definition. If the smaller explanation fails at 4.5:1, darken the text or lighten the background, then verify the new ratio.

The banner also uses an exclamation icon. That icon is non-text, so evaluate whether it is needed to identify the warning and whether other relevant criteria apply. If the word “Warning” already communicates the status, the icon may be supplementary, but it should not introduce a conflicting message. A contrast check and a non-color cue answer different questions.

When assessing an image of text, measure its text against its background too unless an exception applies. Replacing it with real text may improve adaptability and address 1.4.5, but it does not remove the need for readable contrast. Keep evidence for the original and corrected states so another reviewer can reproduce the result.

## Required lesson activity

Select a page and inventory five text/background pairs, including a link and an error state. Measure each with a contrast tool, record font size and weight, identify the applicable threshold or exception, and revise failures. Repeat the measurement after the change and document any background that varies.

## Mini-assessment

1. What is the normal-text threshold? Answer: At least 4.5:1, unless an applicable exception applies.
2. A measurement is 4.49:1. Does rounding make it pass? Answer: No.
3. What threshold generally applies to qualifying large-scale text? Answer: At least 3:1.
