# 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast

## Learning objectives

Identify visual information needed to recognize controls, states, and meaningful graphics, then measure its contrast against adjacent colors. WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.11 is Level AA; read the [full criterion](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#non-text-contrast).

## Contrast for controls and graphics

Visual information required to identify user interface components and their states must generally have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colors. Parts of graphical objects needed to understand content also require at least 3:1, except where a particular presentation is essential. Inactive components and components whose appearance is determined by the user agent and not modified by the author are excluded under the control provision.

For a checkbox, inspect the boundary and checked indicator against adjacent colors. If a button is identifiable through a high-contrast text label and other visible cues, analyze which visual information is actually necessary to identify it; do not apply a blanket rule to every decorative edge. For a line chart, distinguish lines from the background and from adjacent meaningful marks. Labels or patterns may offer additional distinctions. Text itself is evaluated under the text contrast criteria, while focus appearance has related requirements of its own.

Measure colors in the actual rendered state, including hover, focus, selected and error states when the visual information is needed. Gradients, transparency and anti-aliasing require careful sampling and interpretation; automated checks help but do not replace examining what users must distinguish.

## Identify the information a user needs

First identify the visual information required to recognize a component or its state, and the graphical parts required to understand content. Measure those features against adjacent colors. If a border is the only way to locate an empty input on a white page, a faint border may prevent the user from finding the field. A decorative shadow that conveys no required information is a different case.

For a checkbox, inspect the unchecked boundary and the checked indicator in their relevant states. If the check mark is the cue that shows selection, measure it against the color next to it. Examine focus indicators too, and apply related focus requirements where relevant. A contrasting text label does not automatically make a required visual state discernible.

## Worked scenario: form and chart

A form has a pale gray input outline on white. The outline measures 1.7:1 against the surrounding white. If it is necessary to identify the input, strengthen the outline or provide another sufficiently contrasting visual indication, then remeasure. Associate the label with the input and test the invalid and focused states. A programmatic label and visual contrast solve related but different problems.

A chart uses two lines on a white background. One line is dark and the other very pale. If both lines are needed to understand the data, examine the required graphical parts against adjacent colors. Direct labels and distinct line patterns can help users distinguish the series without relying solely on color. Provide an appropriate text alternative or data summary where needed under other criteria. Do not assume that changing the legend alone makes the lines themselves visible.

## Measurement and exceptions

Measure the actual rendered colors, including relevant transparency, gradients, and state changes. At boundaries, consider the colors adjacent to the meaningful feature instead of selecting a convenient color elsewhere on the page. Record the feature, state, adjacent colors, ratio, and why the feature is needed. Automated tools help find candidates, but a reviewer must determine which visual details carry meaning.

Inactive components and components whose appearance is determined by the user agent and not modified by the author are excluded under the user-interface provision. Check whether a control is genuinely inactive and whether its appearance has been modified. The graphical-object provision also has an exception where a particular presentation is essential. Apply the exact wording to the situation instead of assuming an entire chart is exempt. Text itself is assessed under the text contrast criteria.

## Compare control states

Consider a toggle whose off state is a gray thumb on a pale track and whose on state changes the track to green. Determine which visible features are needed to recognize the control and its current state. Measure the necessary features against adjacent colors in both states. If the only difference is color, add a non-color indication such as a visible label or a position change that can be perceived, and expose the state programmatically. The use-of-color criterion and non-text contrast criterion must each be evaluated on their own terms.

A focus ring may touch a white card on one side and a dark navigation bar on another. Measure the meaningful ring against the colors it actually adjoins in each context. A token that works on one background may fail on another. Check focused buttons, links, checkboxes, and custom controls. Whether a focus indicator meets 1.4.11 is not the only question: other focus-related criteria may impose additional requirements.

## Example: provide a clear boundary

```css
input[type="text"] {
  color: #111;
  background: #fff;
  border: 2px solid #555;
}
```

This example gives the field a visible border, but verify its actual ratio against adjacent backgrounds and check every state in the final interface. A border color can be overridden by a component style, a disabled state, or a high-contrast mode. The label and entered text have their own text-contrast considerations. If the component has an error icon, determine whether the icon is necessary to identify the error and whether an adequate text message is also present.

Report the evidence in terms of user need: “The unchecked box boundary is needed to locate the control and measured below 3:1 against its background.” That is more useful than a generic assertion that a border looks faint. Recheck after the repair and include the relevant rendered state in the record.

## Required lesson activity

Audit a form checkbox and a two-series chart. Identify the visual features necessary to understand each, measure their contrast with adjacent colors, and repair any insufficient contrast. Document the relevant state and why each measured feature matters.

## Mini-assessment

1. What contrast ratio generally applies to required non-text visual information?
2. Are inactive components covered by the component provision?
3. Must every decorative border automatically meet 3:1 under this criterion?

**Answer guide:** 1. At least 3:1. 2. They are excluded. 3. No; evaluate information required to identify the component or state.
