# P2: Alternatives for audio and video

## Learning objectives

Identify information conveyed through sound and moving images; distinguish captions, transcripts, audio description, and sign-language interpretation; and select alternatives for prerecorded and live media using the applicable WCAG 2.2 criteria and exceptions.

## Why media needs more than one alternative

A video can communicate through narration, music, sound effects, visible text, gestures, and action on screen. People do not all receive those channels in the same way. A deaf viewer may need speech and meaningful sounds in captions. A blind viewer may hear the narration but miss a diagram that appears without explanation. A transcript can make material available as text that users can read, search, enlarge, or access using assistive technology. These approaches overlap, but they are not interchangeable: captions do not normally describe all visual action, and audio description does not replace captions.

First identify the media type. Audio-only contains sound without synchronized video; video-only contains moving images without synchronized audio; synchronized media combines audio and video. Whether the presentation is prerecorded or live matters too. Then identify which information is conveyed by each channel. A training video that says “select this option” while highlighting an unlabeled item conveys a necessary instruction visually, even though it contains speech.

## Prerecorded audio-only and video-only

Under 1.2.1 Audio-only and Video-only (Prerecorded), Level A, prerecorded audio-only normally needs an alternative for time-based media that conveys equivalent information. A transcript for an interview should include meaningful speaker changes and relevant sound information as well as spoken words. A transcript that omits the question asked by the interviewer changes the meaning. Review automated transcripts for names, technical terms, and other errors.

For prerecorded video-only, the criterion permits either an alternative for time-based media or an audio track that communicates equivalent information. A silent demonstration of a form submission may need a textual account of the steps and outcome. If the video is a clearly identified media alternative for text, an exception in the criterion may apply. Determine whether the surrounding text really is the content the media supplements; a label alone does not make an unrelated video an alternative.

## Captions: presenting sound as text

Captions for prerecorded synchronized media are covered by 1.2.2, Level A, while 1.2.4 addresses live captions at Level AA. Captions should communicate dialogue and relevant non-speech audio, such as a warning tone or a change of speaker when needed for understanding. Timing matters: a caption shown long after a speaker acts can make the relationship between words and action unclear. A transcript alone is not synchronized captions.

Consider a lecture recording where a bell sounds and the instructor says, “That means the test is complete.” If captions include the words but omit the bell, a viewer may miss what “that” refers to. A useful caption could identify the bell where it occurs. Avoid transcribing irrelevant background noise indiscriminately; the goal is to convey information that matters to understanding the media. Automatically generated captions are a starting point for review, not evidence of accuracy. Check technical vocabulary, punctuation, speaker identity, and synchronization.

## Making visual information available

Audio description communicates important visual information that the main soundtrack does not already provide. Under 1.2.3, Level A, prerecorded synchronized media needs audio description or an alternative for time-based media, subject to the criterion's exception. Under 1.2.5, Level AA, audio description is required for prerecorded synchronized media, subject to its exception. The Level A choice of a media alternative should not be mistaken for satisfying the separate Level AA audio-description criterion.

Imagine a tutorial in which a presenter silently drags a file into a folder while music plays. Captions can identify the music, but they do not necessarily tell a blind listener which file moved or where it went. Audio description could explain that action during a suitable pause. If the original narration already explains the action clearly, duplicating it is not useful. Plan the script and visuals together so that important information is spoken naturally when possible, then review the finished media for information still missing from the soundtrack.

## Additional Level AAA alternatives

The remaining media criteria cover additional situations. 1.2.6 requires sign-language interpretation for prerecorded audio in synchronized media. 1.2.7 addresses extended audio description when ordinary pauses are insufficient to convey the visual information. 1.2.8 requires a media alternative for prerecorded synchronized media and prerecorded video-only media. 1.2.9 requires an alternative that presents equivalent information for live audio-only content. These are Level AAA criteria. Their exact applicability, exceptions, and definitions should be checked in the [WCAG 2.2 specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#time-based-media).

An extended description may pause the media to provide enough time for essential information. A media alternative can include a structured account of dialogue, meaningful sounds, and visual events. Sign language and captions are distinct forms of access; do not assume that one is an equivalent substitute for the other in every context. Similarly, the existence of an alternative does not establish that its content is accurate or complete.

## Compare two approaches

A recorded onboarding video shows an error message while the presenter says, “If this happens, try again.” Adding captions of that sentence helps users access the speech but does not identify the visible error. A stronger production describes the error in narration or supplies audio description, and provides accurate synchronized captions for the audio. Test the result with the screen unavailable and again with sound unavailable. Ask what a viewer would need in each case to complete the task.

## Knowledge check

1. What information do captions communicate that an audio description normally does not? 2. A silent prerecorded video demonstrates a process. What kinds of alternative does 1.2.1 permit? 3. Why can captions alone be insufficient for a video that silently highlights a menu option?

Suggested answers: Captions convey speech and relevant non-speech audio in synchronized text. For prerecorded video-only, 1.2.1 permits an alternative for time-based media or an equivalent audio track, subject to its exception. The highlighted option is visual information, so it needs to be communicated through an appropriate visual-information alternative as well.

## Practical activity

Choose a short training video. Create a two-column observation log in your own notes: one column for information in sound and another for information conveyed visually. Identify anything a listener or viewer would miss when one channel is unavailable. Draft captions for a short excerpt and a description for one important visual action. Review timing and meaning with another student. Finally, identify which criteria apply to the actual media type and whether any stated exception applies. Do not claim full conformance from this exercise alone.

## Key takeaways

Identify media type and timing before selecting alternatives. Captions communicate audio; audio description communicates relevant visual information; transcripts and other media alternatives can provide equivalent information in text. WCAG sets different requirements for prerecorded and live media and for different conformance levels. Review accuracy and the complete criteria, including their exceptions.
